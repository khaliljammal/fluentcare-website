A language-access workflow should help staff recognise a language need and reach the right support. Ask which language a person prefers to speak and read, record that preference, and make the interpreter process easy to find. AHRQ recommends these steps in its [Address Language Differences guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool9.html).

## An illustrative front-desk scenario

A person arrives for an appointment and speaks little English. The receptionist can see the appointment but does not know the person’s preferred language or who can help.

In this example, the first task is to establish the preferred language and follow the clinic’s interpreter process. The receptionist should not have to improvise a policy while a queue builds behind them.

A practical internal prompt might be: “Preferred spoken language confirmed? Interpreter contact available? Clinical team informed?” This is an example of a handoff checklist, rather than a clinical script or a complete policy.

## Make the process available where work happens

Put your clinic’s approved interpreter contact and escalation instructions where staff can find them. A process kept only in a manager’s inbox is difficult to use during check-in.

Consider who staff should contact when the usual interpreter route is unavailable. Make responsibility clear: who requests support, who informs the clinician and who checks that the handoff happened? Review that process with the people who actually answer the phone and receive visitors.

Your local policy should decide what staff can handle and when to escalate. This guide does not establish legal requirements or approve a particular communication method.

## Look beyond the appointment itself

Communication continues through scheduling, billing and follow-up. AHRQ’s guidance includes administrative parts of care in language-access planning, rather than focusing only on the clinician’s conversation.

Use that wider view when reviewing your own workflow. Identify the places where someone has to ask for help twice, where language preference disappears between teams, or where a translated message has no clear contact for questions.

For an internal workshop, choose one fictional journey: booking a visit, arriving, arranging the next appointment. Ask each team how it would reach its approved language support. Write down the gaps before choosing another tool.

## Distinguish spoken and written work

A spoken conversation and a written document need different workflows. AHRQ distinguishes interpreters, who work with speech, from translators, who work with written material.

Keep the distinction clear in your tool evaluation. A voice app demonstration does not establish that a written consent form has been appropriately translated. A translated document does not establish that someone has understood a spoken clinical explanation.

## Evaluate a tool with a narrow question

Decide what question an evaluation should answer. For a release test, that might be whether two staff members can find the pause control or hear the audio in a typical reception area. Use invented details and write down what was observed.

Do not turn a successful demonstration into a claim of clinical accuracy. A tool may feel easy to use while still needing language evaluation, provider agreements or a different escalation process.

Finish the workflow review with an actionable result: an accessible interpreter contact, a clearer owner for requests, or a documented handoff that staff can follow. Assign a person to confirm that the change works in the clinic’s actual process.
